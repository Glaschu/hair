Eight defects, found and fixed

Three passes over this design — two constructive, one adversarial — turned up more in the existing Cloudflare service than in the Firestore plan. Every row below was checked in the code, not inferred.

All eight are now fixed, each with a test that names the loss it prevents. The suite went from 32 to 41. That matters beyond the bug count: the Firestore design inherits these same hazards, so a fix with a test here is a specification there — and the tests are the beginning of the failure drill this document has been promising.

What changed in the service

Claiming a slot, counting it against the link and writing the booking are now one batch, with the loser of a race cleaning up after itself. Links carry a pinned, content-addressed policy, so the server decides what was agreed rather than believing a checkbox. A request id makes a retry return the original booking. Minting a link rejects overlaps with every other live link. Money is integer pence. Times carry their zone. Reset revokes. And ?all=1 means no booking can ever become unreachable.

Settled — the default wording, two days notice

On a £90 service with her 50% deposit of £45, the "remaining appointment cost" is the other £45, so the arithmetic is:

Two days or more — no charge, the whole £45 comes back. Inside two days — 50% of the remaining £45 is £22.50, so she keeps £22.50 and refunds £22.50. Inside 24 hours — the full amount, so nothing comes back.

The two denominators (percent of the deposit, percent of the remaining cost) happen to agree in the middle tier, and nothing but a test keeps them agreeing — so there is one, asserting pounds rather than percentages.

The one thing the service cannot do

Inside 24 hours the prose says the full amount — £90 against a £45 deposit. The service can stop money going back; it cannot reach out and take more. Anything beyond the deposit is between her and the client, and the app has to say so rather than implying it collected.

Built this pass

Cancelling, and why status is the authority

An hour that can never be resold is a lost booking just as surely as one that never arrived. slots.taken was one-way, so a client who booked by mistake and rang to apologise destroyed that Saturday slot for good.

Freeing a slot is also the exact move that opens the window for a stale write to reinstate a dead appointment — the counterexample that broke revision 3's offline claim. So the two are the same problem, and the small version is worth solving first because it is cheap and testable.

The rule

bookings.status is the single authority on whether a booking is live — never the slot. Releasing the hour and marking the booking cancelled happen in one batch, a second cancellation is a no-op rather than a second release, and nothing that could revive a booking may act without consulting the status first.

That is the same test the Firestore resolver needs, written small.

What it does now

  • Either side can cancel. She cancels from the app; the client cancels from the page they booked on, with a token good for that one booking and nothing else. No account, no login — burying cancellation behind "message the salon" is how a no-show happens instead, and a freed hour is worth more to her than an awkward conversation avoided.
  • The client is told the cost before pressing anything. A preview returns the tier that would apply right now. Nobody should discover the charge afterwards.
  • The tier is computed from server time and frozen. Never a device clock, and never recomputed later — the tier that applied is a fact about that moment.
  • A cancelled booking is never replayed as live. The request-id short-circuit only fires for a booking that still stands, or a retry would tell someone they are booked when they are not.

The policy as data, not prose

[ { minNoticeHours: 48, refundPct: 100 },
  { minNoticeHours: 24, refundPct:  50 },
  { minNoticeHours:  0, refundPct:   0 } ]

Structured because the shipped wording is ambiguous in a way that will be argued about, and because the refund maths and the words must never drift apart. Boundaries go the client's way — exactly 48 hours is the 48-hour tier — and the tests check just inside and just outside each one, from real offsets rather than calendar days. "Two days out at 11am" can be 36 hours away depending on the time of day, which is exactly the kind of reasoning that should never decide someone's refund.

These numbers are still her stated terms as I read them. The wording decision below changes them, and changing them is now editing three integers rather than rewriting refund code.

The guarantee

What we can actually promise

Revision 1 claimed "every succeeded payment ends with a confirmed appointment". That is not achievable and should not be promised: a payment can succeed for a slot that no longer exists, and no amount of transactional care prevents it, because the money commits in a system we don't control. Promising it means either lying or silently dropping money.

The invariant, second attempt

Classification. Every succeeded payment carries exactly one current classification on a monotonic ladder — pending → confirmed → {reversed, refunded, partially_refunded, refund_failed} → closed — and every transition is recorded with its cause.

Liveness. No payment sits in a classification that is neither confirmed nor closed for more than 24 hours without a human being told.

Every appointment created by an online deposit has exactly one succeeded, non-reversed payment whose amount and currency match the figure frozen at hold time.

It is running

https://jessie-salon.web.app — the booking page, the pairing page and the API, all on one origin, with rules and indexes deployed and the database refusing strangers.

One thing learned the hard way: a deploy can report "file upload complete" and still not have released. The first attempt failed at the very end on an unrelated cleanup policy, which aborted the hosting release — the files were uploaded, the site served "Site Not Found", and the log read like success. Upload complete is not release complete.

The Firestore API fixes a hazard rather than porting it

On the Worker, claiming a slot and writing the booking were two statements — a process dying between them burned the hour with no booking behind it, which is one of the eight defects found earlier and fixed with a batch.

In Firestore a link and its slots are one document, so the claim, the count and the booking are a single transaction. The hazard stops being something to remember and becomes something the database will not let you do. Two people going for the same hour is now a test that passes because of the storage model, not because of care.

A soundness problem, found and fixed

The transaction wrappers — the resolver, the webhook handler, the outbox drainer — were written against the client SDK and tested against it. Cloud Functions run the Admin SDK. So the code 33 tests were proving was not the code that would ship, and the two differ in ways that matter: snap.exists is a property in the Admin SDK and a method in the client one.

A entirely green suite would have thrown on the first real request. All three are now written and tested against the Admin SDK, pointed at the emulator through FIRESTORE_EMULATOR_HOST. The pure logic — 94 tests across the decision, the classifier, the outbox state machine and the reconciler — never touched an SDK and did not change.

Built and tested this pass — 41 tests

The classifier and the webhook handler exist, with Stripe stubbed at the re-fetch boundary. Every ordering hazard the red team named now has a test: a refund overtaking a confirmation, five concurrent deliveries of one event, a completed-but-unpaid session, money arriving late, money failing late, a dispute that a redelivered completion cannot undo, and a payment whose booking has vanished.

The wire format is no longer an assumption. src/stripeShape.mjs maps what Stripe actually returns onto the shape the classifier reads, and its fixtures are real responses captured from a live test account rather than shapes invented to match the code. Three claims this document had only asserted are now checked: a session's expires_at defaults to exactly 24 hours, the floor really is 30 minutes, and a PaymentIntent carries no client_reference_id — so the booking id must go in payment_intent_data.metadata or half the reconciler's sweep cannot be mapped back to a booking.

The event flow is drilled too. No Stripe CLI was available, so payloads are signed with Stripe's own scheme — which suited the drill better, because it lets a refund be delivered before its confirmation and a secret be rotated mid-flight exactly rather than approximately. src/stripeWebhook.mjs is the door; eventDrill.test.mjs runs signed payload → door → handler → database.

The row worth calling out: a botched secret rotation shows up as refusals, not silence. That is the outage that reads as "nobody is booking", and it is why the design alarms on signatureFailures > 0 rather than waiting for someone to notice an empty diary.

Why revision 3's version had to go

It said "exactly one terminal state", and the failure drill in this same document refutes it: a payment that is confirmed and then disputed has occupied two. Confirmed is not terminal — money can move for months afterwards.

Worse, refunded was being treated as guaranteed-reachable. Stripe refunds can fail asynchronously, days later, against a closed account. The outbox says "refund issued, done", the stylist still has the money, and the client believes they were refunded. That is why refund_failed is a first-class classification that alarms, not an error swallowed by a retry loop.

Two details carry the weight. The third terminal state is where money that cannot become an appointment goes, with a bounded and visible lifetime — without it you are pretending the failure can't happen. And the second clause is scoped to origin: 'online_deposit', so the walk-ins and phone bookings she types in herself don't trip it. A reconciler that cries wolf on day one is a reconciler she stops reading.

The correction that matters most

Overlap is not a document-id collision

Revision 1 said the slot is claimed by create holds/{slotId}, and called that the double-booking guard. It isn't. That guarantees one hold per slot id. It does nothing about a 90-minute colour at 11:00 and a 45-minute cut at 12:00 — different ids, both creates succeed, overlapping appointments. Firestore has no interval constraint.

Worse: her manually entered appointments live in the app. If occupancy is only computed from online holds, the booking page will happily sell the hour she blocked out for the school run.

One occupancy document per day, authoritative for every origin

salons/{salonId}/days/{2026-10-03}
{
  version: 41,                 // compare-and-swap counter
  tz: "Europe/London",
  turnaroundMins: 10,
  intervals: [
    { id:"ap_7c3", s:1759482000000, e:1759489200000, kind:"appointment", origin:"online_deposit" },
    { id:"hd_92a", s:1759492800000, e:1759496400000, kind:"hold",        expiresAt:1759493700000 }
  ]
}
  • Holds and appointments share one array, so the overlap check is a single document read inside a single transaction. Fifteen intervals a day is a few hundred bytes, against a documented 1 MiB document limit.
  • Revision 2 justified this partly against a "one write per second per document" figure. That number is not in the current documentation — the best-practices page declines to give one, saying the maximum rate "depends highly on the workload". The honest justification is the documented limits that do exist (1 MiB per document, 10 MiB per request, 270 seconds per transaction) plus the observation that one stylist generates single-digit writes per day to any given day-document. For multi-tenant this becomes a contention question to measure, not to quote.
  • Turnaround padding is applied to the stored interval, not at query time, so every reader agrees about what is free.
  • Expiry is evaluated at read time, not only by the sweeper. An expiresAt in the past means free whether or not the ten-minute sweep has run. The sweeper only tidies. A sweeper outage must never be able to block bookings, and revision 1 made it a correctness dependency.
The hazard revision 1 waved at

Offline writes, and why she never writes occupancy

Saturday, no signal at the basin, she books Mrs Kerr into 11:00 next week. Twenty minutes later the booking page sells an overlapping slot with a £45 deposit. At closing time her phone gets signal and the queued write replays.

Under plain last-write-wins one of two things happens silently: the paying client's confirmed appointment disappears with the money taken, or her write vanishes and she turns up expecting Mrs Kerr. Firestore replays queued writes with no awareness of elapsed time, and the documentation is explicit that "transactions will fail when the client is offline" — so the transactional guarantee is unavailable on exactly the path that needs it.

1

Client devices never write occupancy. They write intents.

Her app creates an immutable, create-only intents/{id} document carrying the target interval, the sealed payload, the day-document version her device last saw, and a serverTimestamp() that resolves when the write lands — which is what makes elapsed offline time measurable at all.

2

A resolver applies it in a transaction, and can refuse

Three tests in order. Conflict: does it overlap a live interval? → rejected. Staleness: landed more than six hours after it was made, or too many versions behind, or targets a time already past? → needs review, even with no conflict. A write from a device that has been dark for three days describes a world that no longer exists. Otherwise: apply and bump the version.

3

Rejection is a place in the calendar, not an error toast

Her appointment shows the instant she taps — that is the whole point of offline — rendered provisional until it resolves. On rejection it becomes a conflict card in the same spot: "11:00 Saturday went to an online booking while you were offline. Mrs Kerr isn't booked." with two buttons: Move to 12:30 and Keep Mrs Kerr, refund the online booking. Never a dialog that can be dismissed into nothing.

Who wins by default — hold, don't destroy

Revision 3 said the paid online booking wins outright. That is a weapon anyone can buy for £45: pay deposits across her best Saturday slots, watch her real bookings get auto-rejected, then dispute every charge. Each dispute costs her a non-refundable fee whether she wins or not, and links are bearer URLs with no identity binding.

So: the online booking keeps the money and the slot provisionally, her booking is not deleted, and the conflict card is a decision with a deadline — if she doesn't answer within N hours, the online booking wins. "A stranger has been charged" is a reason not to destroy the payment; it was never a reason to destroy her booking first.

With caps to match: one live deposit per client reference, a few per link, and a daily ceiling across the salon. For one chair those limits are tiny and a real client never reaches them.

For her non-occupancy edits — notes, prices, service definitions — security rules enforce compare-and-swap directly, which converts last-write-wins into CAS in one line:

allow update: if isStylist()
  && request.resource.data.version == resource.data.version + 1;

A stale queued write carrying version: 42 that replays after the server reached 45 is rejected by the rules and rolled back locally, instead of quietly winning.

Settled: use the native SDK

@react-native-firebase/firestore documents persistence as on by default, with an explicit opt-out. The Firebase JS SDK still has no durable persistence under React Native — persistentLocalCache is IndexedDB-backed, React Native has no IndexedDB, and the upstream feature request for it is still open. So the JS SDK falls back to a memory cache that dies with the session.

But the one property we are actually betting on — queued writes surviving a force-quit — is documented nowhere, by anyone. The drill decides it, and a pass is a local empirical result pinned to a library version, not a platform guarantee. Re-run it on every major upgrade.

The residual race, stated honestly

Her offline device cannot publish its intent to the booking page's availability check, because it is offline. No client-side design eliminates this. It can only be made small: links offer specific slots she chose rather than open availability, holds are short, intents publish the instant there is any signal, and the conflict path is a two-tap resolution rather than a corruption. A design claiming to have eliminated it would be lying.

Money

The Stripe rules that actually prevent loss

"Only the webhook confirms" is right but nowhere near sufficient. The specifics:

  • checkout.session.completed is authoritative only when payment_status == 'paid'. Delayed methods fire it as unpaid and settle later. Enumerate payment_method_types explicitly rather than using automatic methods, and handle the async success/failure pair regardless.
  • Re-fetch, never trust the payload. On every webhook, ignore the event's embedded object and retrieve it fresh from the API, then compute state from current truth. One extra call per event at her volume, and most ordering bugs vanish — a refund that overtakes a confirmation can no longer produce a confirmation.
  • payment_intent.succeeded carries no client_reference_id. Set payment_intent_data.metadata too, or the reconciler — which walks PaymentIntents — can't map half of them.
  • Monotonic state machine. Rank the states and drop any transition that would move backwards, recording it rather than applying it.
  • Read the idempotency marker first, inside the same transaction as the effect. Writing it first as a claim means a crash between claim and work permanently suppresses a real event. Losing the race returns HTTP 200 — returning 500 makes Stripe retry into the same race, which is an amplification loop that looks like an outage.
  • Nothing may happen after res.send(). Function instances are frozen the instant the response goes. The transaction and the 200 are the handler's only effects; everything else is an outbox row.
The paid booking that would never have arrived

Two fields, two meanings: classification is what the money did, status is what the booking is. The webhook handler moved the first and never the second, so a confirmed payment left the booking at pending_payment — and the inbox her device reads asks for status == 'confirmed'.

Money taken, appointment never delivered, nobody told. The exact outcome this design exists to prevent, sitting in the deployed service. Status now follows the classification, but only ever out of pending_payment, so a late webhook cannot resurrect a booking she already cancelled.

Beneath it, a second one: the deposit path never stored the sealed envelope, so even once fixed she would have received an appointment with no name. It has to be captured before the money moves — the webhook arrives long after the page holding those details has closed.

A secret that was set, and still invisible

The Stripe key was in Secret Manager, correctly set, and the deposit route still answered 501. A v2 function only reads secrets it declares: without secrets: ['STRIPE_SECRET_KEY'] in the options, process.env.STRIPE_SECRET_KEY is undefined and the route behaves exactly as though no key had ever been set. Nothing anywhere says why.

The proof it is bound is not the capabilities line — it is the route answering 404 expired for an unknown link instead of 501, which means the client was built and the lookup ran. Two preflight checks now hold it: that the function declares the secret, and that a service advertising deposits is not simultaneously answering 501 on the deposit route.

The last few feet, closed

A booking made on the web now lands in her diary on a pull-down. The commit goes straight to SQLite, not through React state: the service is told a booking is safe the moment apply resolves, and a state setter resolves when React has been asked to re-render — a different and much earlier moment, and the exact window the ordering exists to close.

One function knows there are two deployments. It caught a fresh bug: on Firebase it fell through to the HTTP inbox, and there is no /api/bookings there — only the public booking routes are Functions. Silent 404s on every refresh, reported as "bookings never arrive".

A booking that could never be cleared

Her device clears the inbox by writing seenAt. The rules enforce compare-and-swap — every edit of hers must carry version + 1 — and that expression does not evaluate to 1 on a document with no version, it errors, and an erroring rule denies. Neither writer was stamping a version, so every booking was one she could never clear: redelivered forever, with nothing reporting a fault. Proven in the emulator, fixed at both writers, pinned by four rules tests.

The inbox itself is at-least-once delivery applied idempotently: the diary is written before the booking is acknowledged, so the failure mode is a duplicate delivery rather than a lost Saturday. The local id is derived from the service's id, so a re-delivery collides with itself instead of becoming two appointments — dedupe that survives a restart and a restore from backup.

Deployed, and switched off

The deposit route is live and answers 501 — stripeClient() returns null without a key, so the route ships inert rather than present and broken. Turning deposits on becomes one secret rather than a deploy.

Two encoding details that are silently expensive: Stripe wants nested objects as a[b][c]=v, and the idempotency key is a header, not a field — as a field it is ignored, every retry becomes a new session, and that is a second charge.

Built this pass — 16 tests, no live key needed

src/deposit.mjs creates the Checkout Session and holds the slot. Four decisions carry it: the session is created before the hold is written, so a refusal from Stripe costs no chair time; expires_at is never left to default, because Stripe's default would hold a Saturday morning until Sunday; held is not taken, since only a signed webhook takes a slot; and holds lapse by being read, so an outage in the sweeper can never block a booking.

The one that actually loses money

The hold expires while the client is still paying. Fixes in order of prevention: derive the hold's TTL from the Checkout Session's own expiry rather than picking one independently; make checkout.session.expired the primary release trigger with the sweeper as backstop; and when it happens anyway, the confirm handler finds no free interval, writes held_for_review: slot_lost, and enqueues a refund keyed to the PaymentIntent. The app then says: "Someone paid £45 for Saturday 11:00 but the slot went. Refunded automatically — you may want to call them."

Auto-refund is the default because it never leaves a stranger out of pocket, but it should be configurable: for a stylist who is fully booked, the better answer is often keep the money, phone them, move them to Tuesday.

Stripe's idempotency key expires before its own retries stop

Idempotency keys may be pruned after 24 hours. Webhooks retry for three days. So a refund sitting in the outbox and retried on day two presents a pruned key, and Stripe creates a second refund.

The rule that falls out: the outbox's own marker in the database is the durable guard, not the Stripe key. Stripe's key protects the first day only. Anything that moves money checks our own ledger before it calls out.

Two defaults that are hostile

  • Checkout Sessions expire in 24 hours unless told otherwise, and the floor is 30 minutes. Leave it unset and a Saturday-morning slot is held all day by someone who wandered off. Set it explicitly at the floor and derive the hold from it.
  • Firestore TTL deletes "typically within 24 hours", and expired documents keep appearing in queries until the sweep actually runs. TTL therefore cannot release a 30-minute hold. This makes revision 2's read-time expiry rule load-bearing rather than defensive — TTL is a cost optimisation, never a correctness mechanism.

One more worth designing around: Checkout waits up to ten seconds for the webhook response before redirecting the client. A slow handler makes a paying customer stare at a spinner, which is a second reason nothing may happen after the response goes.

Freeze the amount, and put the policy in the record

  • Freeze expectedAmountMinor, currency and price version onto the booking at hold time. Confirm compares against the frozen value, never the live service price — otherwise editing a price mid-session silently accepts the wrong money.
  • Every timestamp a decision depends on is a server timestamp. Device clocks write advisory metadata only.
  • agreedAt is currently useless as evidence — a timestamp with no record of what was agreed. Edit the policy and it points at nothing. Store a policyHash of the exact text shown, with an immutable policy document behind it. That is the single artefact that wins a chargeback.
  • Store the policy as structured tiers and generate the prose from it, so the words and the refund maths cannot drift apart.

What's sealed and what's clear

A claim from revision 1 that was too strong

"Random clientRef per link so the backend can't correlate visits" is true of the booking links and false of the synced appointment history — stable document ids let the backend see this anonymous person comes every six weeks and spends £90.

Fix: derive client document ids as HMAC(salonKey, clientId) — stable across her devices, which share the key, opaque to the backend, and uncorrelatable between salons. Then the honest claim is: cannot identify anyone, cannot link a client across salons, can see that some anonymous regular exists.

The envelope is attacker-supplied, and unbound

Two properties of the current format that the design has been assuming away:

  • Anyone who can load a booking page can seal anything. A sealed box authenticates to an attacker-chosen ephemeral key, so "authenticated encryption" guarantees nothing about authorship. The decrypted text goes straight into client.name, phone and notes — including, on the allergies field, somewhere with real safety consequences. Treat an import as a proposal: strip control characters and bidi overrides, clamp lengths, normalise the phone before it reaches a sms: URL, and let her confirm rather than writing straight through.
  • Deleting attacker text doesn't stick. The field-level merge deliberately backfills blanks from the older record — which is right for a missing Instagram handle and wrong here: clear an abusive note on one device and the other restores it on the next sync. Needs a suppressed tombstone the merge honours.
  • The envelope isn't bound to its booking. It contains only the personal fields — no token, slot, time, price or policy id. A compromised or merely buggy backend can attach any envelope to any booking, or swap two clients' allergy notes, and the app renders it as truth. Seal the identifying context alongside the personal data and refuse the import when the sealed fields disagree with the clear ones. That is a few lines, and it buys a replay guard and the kid hook the keyring already wants.
Rules

Keeping strangers out of the database entirely

The premise that removes a whole class of leak: no unauthenticated principal ever touches Firestore. The public booking page talks to the Cloudflare Worker, which writes with a service account. The only clients holding Firestore credentials are her devices. Rules are therefore "deny everything, then allow the stylist a few narrow shapes", not a public API surface.

That is also the argument for keeping the Worker rather than folding the page into Firebase Hosting: it is the only component strangers touch, it is already tested thirty-two ways, and keeping Firestore closed is what makes these rules short.

These now run

The rules are written and 29 tests execute them against the Firestore emulator — firebase/firestore.rules and firebase/rules.test.mjs, in their own package so firebase-tools never enters the app's dependency tree. One case per leak, named for the leak rather than the rule.

They found a lockout on the first run. request.auth.token.suspended throws when the claim is absent, and a thrown condition denies — so adding a claim in a later release would have locked every device still holding an older token out of her own salon. Every claim is now read with .get(name, default). Reading the file would not have caught that.

The single most valuable rule

match /bookings/{b} {
  allow update: if stylist()
    && request.resource.data.diff(resource.data).affectedKeys()
         .hasOnly(['sealed','kid','clientNote','seenAt','version'])
    && request.resource.data.version == resource.data.version + 1;
  allow create, delete: if false;
}

She may annotate a booking; she can never touch status, amountMinor or stripe.*. Worth writing first.

But it does not do what revision 3 claimed

Revision 3 said this rule means "a replayed offline write physically cannot resurrect a cancelled or refunded booking". False, and the counterexample is ordinary: her iPad, offline since Thursday, nudges a confirmed appointment to 11:15. On Friday the client cancels and is refunded, so the interval is removed from the day document. The iPad reconnects. The intent's conflict test passes — 11:15 is free now — staleness passes, past-time passes. The resolver applies it and the refunded appointment is back in her calendar and back in occupancy.

The rule was never involved: her device wrote an intent, which is create-only and therefore allowed, and the resolver runs with admin privileges that bypass rules entirely. So the resolver needs a fourth test, run first — resolve the target booking and refuse if its status is anything but live. And the honest claim is: rules stop her device rewriting payment state directly; the resolver is what stops a stale intent reinstating a dead appointment, and the resolver is server code that must be tested, not a rule that is enforced.

Built and tested this pass

The resolver exists: firebase/src/resolve.mjs as a pure decision, with the transaction around it in src/applyIntent.mjs. 38 tests — 31 of them without a database, because a pure decision lets the awkward cases be constructed exactly rather than approximated.

Five statuses are tested for resurrection — cancelled, refunded, reversed, no_show, expired — each with the hour free, the clock fresh and the time still in the future, so every other check waves it through. And the ordering itself is a test: if conflict ran before status, a cancelled booking whose hour was since taken would report "someone beat you to it" rather than "that booking is gone".

How multi-tenant rules leak

  • Collection-group queries route around path rules. A rule at /salons/{sid}/clients/{c} does nothing for collectionGroup('clients'). Default-deny saves you; a permissive group rule written for convenience does not. Denormalise salonId into every document so a group rule can be written safely if ever needed.
  • Rules filter documents, not queries — and people loosen rules to make lists work. Path-based tenancy makes lists naturally scoped and removes the temptation.
  • Custom claims are stale for up to an hour. Revoking access does not take effect in rules until the token refreshes, and revoking refresh tokens doesn't invalidate a live one. Money-moving and destructive operations go through callables that check a live revocation document. Rules are not a revocation mechanism.
  • Anonymous auth satisfies request.auth != null. Enable it anywhere in the project and every such rule is public. Check the sign-in provider too.
  • Callables using the Admin SDK bypass rules entirely. In practice this is where the real leak will be — a callable taking salonId from the request body instead of the token. Every callable derives tenancy from the token. Make it a review checklist item.
  • Exports, backups and BigQuery streaming ignore rules completely.
Why the sealing pays for itself — stated accurately this time

A leaked export exposes times, prices, opaque blobs, and pointers into Stripe. A pi_… resolves in one API call to the name, email, billing address and card last4 that Checkout collected. So the sealing is strong against a stranger with a stolen backup, and weak against anyone who also holds Stripe read access — which in main-hosted mode is the platform operator, the exact party most likely to be subpoenaed or compromised.

Two smaller leaks in the same spirit: links.greeting stores a real first name in the clear, and a first name plus exact recurring appointment times in one town is not "not people". And any server-sent confirmation needs a plaintext address somewhere. Either the outbox holds personal data, or her device does the sending — that is a decision, not an oversight, and it belongs in the table above as its own row.

Correcting revision 1

Key recovery, before the first deposit

Revision 1 said "put the secret key in the iCloud Keychain". That contradicts the shipped code. bookingStore.ts uses AFTER_FIRST_UNLOCK_THIS_DEVICE_ONLY, which does not sync and is not restored onto a replacement device — deliberately, with a comment saying a secret that syncs is a secret in a file. That is a defensible choice. It also means: drop the phone in the Clyde with the iPad un-paired at home, and every sealed envelope in the cloud is gone for good.

The recovery card

A 160-bit code rendered as eight groups of five Crockford base-32 characters — no I, L, O or U, so it survives being handwritten. It wraps the salon secret key with a deliberately slow KDF (pure-JS scrypt, since React Native has no crypto.subtle; target one to two seconds). Only the wrapped blob is uploaded — useless without the code, and the code exists only on paper.

What she sees is one printable card: salon name, date, the code in large type, a QR of the same, and one sentence — "Keep this with your passport. Without it, a lost phone means lost client notes." Not a word list, not a JSON file, not an email to herself.

Built and tested this pass — 18 tests

src/data/recovery.ts. Crockford base32 with no I, L, O or U, because this gets handwritten and read back months later — and normalizeCode forgives the substitutions a person actually makes: an O for a zero, an I or L for a one. Rejecting those would mean telling her the card is wrong when it is right.

scrypt at N=215 measures ~170 ms on a laptop, so pure-JS on her phone lands in the intended one-to-two seconds. A wrong code returns null with no detail about how it was wrong — the difference between "bad code" and "bad blob" is a small oracle and there is nothing useful she could do with it.

canTakeDeposits() is the gate as code rather than a note: a verified card, or two paired devices. The end-to-end test seals a booking, throws the key away, recovers it from the card and opens the envelope.

Two changes that shrink the blast radius

  • A keyring, not a key. The envelope format has no key id today. Don't change the wire format — store kid as a clear field beside the sealed blob, with missing meaning key 1. Old envelopes keep opening after a rotation, which turns re-keying from a data-loss event into an ordinary operation and fixes today's all-or-nothing reset path.
  • Make the cloud envelope transient. When her device imports a booking it re-seals the personal data into the durable record and marks the original superseded. Key loss then costs only the envelopes sealed since the last import — minutes, not the whole history. Cheapest high-value change in this section.

When the key is genuinely gone

The honest answer is also a workable product. The clear fields survive, so she still sees that someone is booked Saturday at 11:00 for a full head of colour, paid £45, agreed to the terms at 20:41. unsealBooking already returns null rather than crashing and the app already carries details: null through to the UI, so the row renders as "Details unavailable — sealed to a key this device no longer has". One tap sends the client a fresh link asking them to re-enter their name and number against the new key.

And the app says it out loud, once, at setup: this is the price of Google not being able to read your clients.

Stripe Connect — the vocabulary changed twice

Revision 2 said "Standard / Express / Custom". Delete that. The type parameter is now deprecated in favour of controller properties — fees.payer, losses.payments, requirement_collection, stripe_dashboard.type. The shape we want (stylist is merchant of record, has her own dashboard, platform never holds her money) turns out to be every one of those defaults, so it is close to a bare account creation plus direct charges.

And the bigger one: there is now an Accounts v2 API, and Stripe steers new Connect platforms to it — the v1 design guide opens by saying it applies only to existing platforms. Main-hosted would be greenfield, so it should start on v2, where the account-type axis is replaced by configurations (merchant / customer / recipient) and defaults.responsibilities.

A precision worth keeping: losses.payments does not mean "who pays disputes". It governs who is liable when the account can't cover a negative balance. Disputes hit her balance because the charge lives on her account — that is a consequence of direct charges, not of the controller setting. Two mechanisms, easily conflated.

Three operational consequences: every API call carries the account header; the webhook dedupes on (account, event id), not event id alone, or one tenant's event silently suppresses another's; and she can refund from her own dashboard, so subscribe to the Connect refund events and let the app say "refunded outside iris" rather than showing a confident, wrong confirmed.

Self-hosted needs none of this. Keep the Stripe adapter behind the same seam as the backend adapter so the Connect complexity lives in exactly one implementation.

The hole revision 2 left

Moving her live data across

She uses this app every working day. The migration has to happen underneath her with no maintenance window, no read-only mode, and no moment where opening the app shows missing clients. And it has to be reversible, because we might be wrong about Firestore.

The shape that gives all three: snapshot, then tail. Copy a consistent snapshot up, then repeatedly sweep rows changed since the snapshot until a sweep comes back empty twice. She keeps booking throughout.

The rule that makes rollback free

No step deletes or overwrites local data. New columns are added beside the old ones, never in place. The pre-migration snapshot file is kept forever. iCloud sync stays running the whole time.

The moment someone proposes dropping the old float columns "since we've moved to pence now", rollback stops being free. That is the line to hold.

Before anything is written

  • The recovery card has been round-tripped, not merely displayed. Migration is the first bulk encryption, so a key that can't be recovered turns her whole history into noise in one pass rather than one booking at a time.
  • Both devices provably hold the same key. Seal a canary on the iPhone, unseal it on the iPad, require green. The keychain class doesn't travel, so this is a real failure mode rather than a theoretical one.
  • Converge the two devices first. Push-pull on each, twice — the first push can't contain rows the other device hasn't uploaded yet. Then compare a manifest of row counts and keyed digests. Unequal manifests keep migration locked.
  • Count the rows against the write ceiling, and be on Blaze before starting.

Deterministic ids, so every write is idempotent

docId(kind, localId) = base32( HMAC-SHA256(salonKey, kind + ":" + localId) )

The same HMAC scheme the privacy section already needs, extended to every collection. It buys three things at once: the backend still can't correlate, both devices independently derive the same id, and the migration becomes resumable — interrupt it at 60%, relaunch, and re-running writes the same ids over the same documents. Never add(), never a generated UUID.

A bonus worth noting, since random-looking ids read like a privacy tax: Firestore's own best-practices page warns that sequential document ids cause hotspots. Uniformly distributed HMAC output is also the faster choice.

One key cannot do both jobs

Deriving ids from the salon key collides with the keyring promise: ids keyed to it can never be rotated, because rotating means every document gets a new id. So "re-keying becomes an ordinary operation" and "ids are HMACs of the salon key" cannot both be true, and one compromise would retroactively destroy unlinkability for the whole dataset with no remedy.

Idempotency needs determinism, not secrecy. Give each row a random remoteId at creation and sync it — resumable, uncorrelatable, key-free, and it survives rotation. Keep HMAC only for the verification digests, under its own derived subkey, and never build a preimage by string concatenation: ("a:b","c") and ("a","b:c") hash identically, and with set() a collision is a silent overwrite rather than a duplicate.

Related: the migrator lease needs a monotonic epoch, not just a heartbeat. An iPad restored from a three-week-old backup can take over a finished migration after 30 minutes and rewrite every document with stale content — and the row counts still match.

Electing one migrator

A local "I am migrating" flag cannot prevent both devices starting, because both can set it offline. The claim is a create-only document, arbitrated by the server, with a heartbeat so a stuck claim can be taken over after 30 minutes. The loser goes into follower mode and shows "Your iPhone is moving your data across. Nothing to do here."

Verification: three checks, three different failures

  1. Counts, extents, and money sums — catches missing rows. Sum every money field in integer pence and require exact equality. Off-by-a-penny is a bug, not rounding, and integers make it visible.
  2. Keyed per-record digests rolled into a per-collection root — catches altered content. Use HMAC(salonKey, …), not a bare hash: a plain SHA-256 of "name|phone" stored beside the record is a confirmation oracle anyone with the database can test guesses against.
  3. The second device re-verifies independently — it pulls everything, unseals with its key, recomputes, and reports without repairing. This is the only check that proves the data is readable by a device that didn't write it.

The Firestore side must be computed by reading documents back and unsealing them — never by hashing what you were about to upload. That is the difference between finding a key mismatch today and finding it in March when she taps a client and gets "Details unavailable". Note that sealing is non-deterministic (fresh ephemeral key and nonce per call), so comparing ciphertext is worthless; hash the plaintext, on device.

Signing in — built this pass, 27 tests

So there are two doors into the same narrow permissions. A custom claim for the hosted deployment, which has a Function anyway. An allowlist document for self-hosting: she signs in, the app shows a user ID, it goes in config/access. That document is unreadable and unwritable from the app — stylist() reads it through get(), which the rules engine evaluates, so the caller never needs access to it. A client-writable allowlist is not an allowlist.

App side: email and password, not the email link originally planned. A link has to reopen the app, which needs a URL scheme in app.json and therefore a native rebuild — and this app has no scheme. Password reaches the same signed-in uid with no native change at all. The seam is shaped so signInWithCustomToken can replace it later: the booking service already proves who she is with a device key.

The state worth designing for is the middle one — signed in but not yet allowed in, which is where every self-hosted first run lands. The app refreshes the token first (permission lives in the token, and claims only change on refresh), then decides by actually reading something rather than guessing. And it never calls that state an error: it is the normal first run, and wording it as a failure would send her looking for a fault that is not there.

A claim checked and rejected

The refinement pass reported that appointment_products and service_products have never synced between her devices — which would have been a serious live data-loss bug. It's wrong.
